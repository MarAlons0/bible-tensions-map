# Bible Reading Notes: Summary and Book-by-Book Recap

*Hebrew Bible, Apocrypha and New Testament, read in the New Oxford Annotated Bible (NRSV). Reading approach: literary, historical and theological analysis, not historical verification. Update this file as the reading continues.*

---

## Part 1. Overall Summary

### Your three closing observations, with an assessment

**1. "The Bible is a Rorschach test."**
Largely supported. The same texts were used to defend and attack slavery, to demand and to forbid political obedience (Romans 13 versus Revelation 13), to support and to oppose women's ordination, and to justify opposite views on predestination, the millennium and images. The text underdetermines doctrine. It isn't "anything goes," though. Some readings need much more interpretive strain than others, and the historical-critical method exists to tell them apart.

**2. "No coherent narrative or ethics; even 'thou shalt not kill' is malleable."**
Half right. The ethics are inconsistent, and the clearest cases are the ones you identified: violence against outsiders is celebrated, while violence against insiders is punished; slavery is accepted; women are subordinated; God commands what other texts forbid. On the commandment itself, the Hebrew verb (*ratsach*) usually means unlawful killing or murder, so the real conflict is less "thou shalt not kill" versus war than *who counts* as protected. That is your in-group/out-group point. A narrative does run through the canon (covenant, kingship, exile and return, hope for restoration), but it was imposed by editors and later readers, not by one author.

**3. "A series of books over a thousand years, each with its own agenda."**
The strongest of the three, and mainstream scholarly consensus. Most contradictions you found (Samuel/Chronicles, Matthew/Luke genealogies, Matthew/Acts on Judas, Job's frame versus its speeches) are what a composite library produces. Believers who read it as one book have to harmonize, and the harmonizations were often the weakest part of what we discussed.

### The main threads we traced

| Theme | Trajectory |
|---|---|
| **Afterlife** | None (Pentateuch) → shadowy Sheol (Psalms, Job) → total cessation (Ecclesiastes) → hints (Isaiah 24-27, Psalm 49) → resurrection (Daniel 12, 2 Maccabees 7) → heaven and hell (2 Esdras) → eternal torment and immortality (Revelation) |
| **Monotheism** | Other gods exist (Exodus, "God of gods," "jealous") → Deuteronomy transitional → explicit denial (Second Isaiah) → Paul still says "many gods" (1 Cor 8:5) |
| **Satan and evil** | Talking serpent → "the satan" as role in Job → "Satan" inciting David (Chronicles) → named demons (Tobit) → cosmic enemy (NT) → identified with the serpent (Revelation 12:9) |
| **Messiah** | "Anointed" = kings and priests → hope for restored David line → Elijah/messenger (Malachi) → "my son the Messiah" (2 Esdras) → Jesus |
| **Christology** | Empowered human agent (Acts 10:38) → pre-existent Word (John) → Hebrews 1 and Revelation's shared titles → Nicaea. Arian and Trinitarian readings both cite the same verses |
| **Holy Spirit** | God's breath/power → "his holy spirit" → personal Paraclete (John) → Trinity (4th century) |
| **Divine character** | Anger/compassion oscillation, changeable God, God hardening hearts, God "setting people up" (Eden to the beast of Revelation 13) |
| **Violence** | Celebrated against outsiders (herem, Maccabees); non-retaliation taught in the NT but retribution deferred to Christ as judge |
| **Women** | Purity laws, silence texts, household codes, "equality after death, hierarchy in life" |
| **Failed expectations** | Matthew 24:34, 1 Corinthians 7, 2 Peter's reply to the delay |

## Part 2. Book-by-Book Recap

### Hebrew Bible

**Genesis**
- The serpent of Genesis 3 is never called Satan; that identification is later (Revelation 12:9).
- Abraham marries his half-sister (20:12), later forbidden in Leviticus.
- Table of Nations (ch. 10) and Babel explain the world's peoples without separate creator gods.
- Circumcision (ch. 17) is given as a covenant sign with no stated rationale; it works as an identity marker.
- Polygamy among the patriarchs goes uncondemned.

**Exodus**
- "Who is like you among the gods?" and "no other gods before me" assume other gods exist.
- God relents after the golden calf when Moses argues.
- Jealousy implies rivals.

**Leviticus**
- Sibling relations are prohibited (18, 20), but Abraham and Jacob's marriages (sisters) show the later date of the law.
- Menstruation and childbirth cause ritual impurity. *Observation: the contradiction between God designing female biology and that biology being impure is never addressed anywhere in the Bible.*
- Sukkot/booths, consecration of houses and animals (ch. 27), the Jubilee (never practiced).
- Orthodox Judaism still keeps Sukkot, but there is no Temple, so no sacrifice or consecration system.

**Numbers**
- Bitter-water ordeal (ch. 5): magical, not toxicological, and applied only to wives.
- Anakim giants: narrative function, not history.
- "Wilderness" as testing ground. A population of 600,000 men is archaeologically absent.
- Bronze serpent (ch. 21), later destroyed as Nehushtan.

**Deuteronomy**
- All rewards and punishments are earthly and generational; no afterlife.
- Centralization of worship explains the later condemnation of "high places."
- Intermarriage ban targets the seven Canaanite nations on religious grounds.
- Non-sacrificial slaughter permitted (ch. 12), not a contradiction of Leviticus.
- Polygamy accepted; polyandry never.
- "God of gods" (10:17) is supremacy language, not exclusive monotheism.

**Joshua and Judges**
- The Caleb/Achsah story appears in both (a doublet).
- Herem (total destruction) is presented as obedience.
- Gideon's blood vengeance for two brothers versus thousands killed in war: no reflection on the double standard.
- Abimelech's murder of 70 brothers on one stone is likely stylized.
- Samson is a failed hero; the story resists a coherent moral.

**1-2 Samuel**
- Anti-monarchical (1 Sam 8) and pro-monarchical (2 Sam 7) strands side by side.
- David executes the Amalekite messenger without verification. We already know from 1 Sam 31 that Saul killed himself, so the "he was lying" defense doesn't rescue David's justice.
- Bathsheba and Uriah (2 Sam 11): the text condemns it, but Bathsheba's agency is invisible.
- David's victory hymn (2 Sam 22 = Psalm 18) claims blamelessness after those events.
- The census: God incites it here, Satan in Chronicles.

**1-2 Kings**
- Stock curse formula ("dogs shall eat...") against condemned dynasties, likely written after the fact.
- Casualty numbers (100,000 killed in a day) are hyperbole.
- Baal-zebub (a mocked Philistine god) becomes the NT's Beelzebul.
- High places were normal YHWH worship sites until the Deuteronomistic reforms.
- Nehushtan: Moses's bronze serpent destroyed by Hezekiah.
- Male cult prostitutes in the Temple (Josiah's reforms) show how mainstream syncretism was.
- Annals formula ("are they not written in the Book of the Annals...?") is a citation convention.
- Solomon's foreign wives are central to his fall here but absent from Chronicles.

**1-2 Chronicles**
- Satan first appears as a named instigator (1 Chron 21:1), replacing God from 2 Sam 24:1.
- Idealizes David and Solomon: omits Bathsheba, the foreign wives and apostasy.
- Why counting people is a sin is never explained; pride and conscription are the leading theories.

**Ezra-Nehemiah**
- Forced divorce of foreign wives is much harsher than Deuteronomy.
- "Jews" as a term arises after the exile because only Judah survived.
- Language loss in mixed families is treated as an identity threat.

**Job**
- The frame story (a wager with "the satan") is incompatible with God's speeches about cosmic mystery.
- Leviathan is a mythological chaos monster, not a whale; in Canaanite myth the storm god defeats it, in Job God created and controls it.
- God vindicates Job's protest over the friends' defense of divine justice.

**Psalms**
- Sheol is passive silence: "in Sheol who can give you praise?"
- "Brought up from Sheol" (Ps 30) is rescue language; the text is ambiguous enough that it can be read more literally.
- Psalm 49 is ambiguous on afterlife; Psalm 51:5 is not original sin.
- Sacrifice is downplayed in favor of a contrite heart, which later enabled worship without the Temple.
- Psalm 117 is universal praise, not evangelism; "awake my soul" (Ps 57:8, 108:2), not Proverbs, is the Mumford & Sons phrase.

**Proverbs**
- The "cistern" passage (ch. 5) is about marital fidelity.
- Numerical sayings ("three things... four") are a poetic device, not counting.

**Ecclesiastes**
- "Nothing new under the sun" is from 1:9.
- "The dead know nothing" (9:5-6) is the clearest statement of finality in the Bible.
- 7:28 ("one man among a thousand... a woman not found") is plain misogyny.
- The silver cord (12:6) is physical death imagery; "a time for everything" became "Turn! Turn! Turn!"
- You found it the most sincere book; it was nearly excluded from the canon.

**Song of Songs**
- No mention of God; "my sister, my bride" is love-poetry convention, not incest.
- Included via Solomonic attribution and allegory.
- Catholic tradition allegorized away the eroticism, which conflicts with its shame-based sexual ethics.

**Isaiah**
- Second Isaiah gives the first explicit monotheism.
- Isaiah 24-27 has the first hints of resurrection ("your dead shall live").
- Chapter 23 is an oracle against Tyre.

**Ezekiel**
- God negotiates the dung used for baking bread (4:12-15).
- Menstruation violations listed among Jerusalem's sins (22:10); Oholah and Oholibah are allegories for Samaria and Jerusalem.
- Water from the Temple (ch. 47) is restoration imagery echoed in Revelation 22.
- Ezekiel 18 rejects inherited guilt.

**Daniel**
- First explicit resurrection with differentiated fates (12:2).
- "Prince of princes" in 8:25 means God, not a Messiah; I was wrong to suggest otherwise.
- Post-facto prophecy of Antiochus IV; "Son of Man" appears in ch. 7.

**Hosea**
- "On the third day he will raise us up" (6:2) refers to national restoration. It may have supplied the "third day" motif for the resurrection narratives; that is plausible but unproven.

**Zephaniah and the prophets on Moab**
- Moab is consistently negative in the text. Moab, Utah (named 1855) probably took the name from landscape or biblical familiarity, not endorsement.

**Malachi**
- The nearest OT approach to hell ("burning like an oven") and to a messianic messenger (Elijah returning).

### Apocrypha

**Tobit**
- Named angel (Raphael) and named demon (Asmodeus), magic, Hades; a folktale genre unlike the canonical OT.
- The sparrow-dropping blindness is medically plausible.

**Sirach**
- "Neither seek what is too difficult for you" (3:21) is conservative anti-speculation advice. Sirach 31 has the closest thing to table-manners rules, relevant to the "elbows" question; no Bible verse says it.

**Baruch and the Letter of Jeremiah**
- "Everlasting joy" is national restoration, not afterlife.
- The anti-idol polemic uses menstrual impurity as a measure of worthlessness.

**1 Maccabees**
- War elephants with towers are a likely source for Tolkien's oliphaunts. Revenge on a wedding party (9:37-41) is narrated approvingly.
- "Gentile" comes from Latin *gentilis*, translating *goyim*/*ethne*.

**2 Maccabees**
- Chapter 7 is the first explicit resurrection theology, driven by martyrdom (the old reward theology had failed).

**4 Maccabees**
- "Reason" means Stoic self-mastery within Torah, not modern empirical reason.

**2 Esdras**
- Written around 100 CE as pseudonymous prophecy. "My son the Messiah" dies after a 400-year reign.
- First clear heaven/hell dualism (7:36), with Persian influence.
- "Holy spirit" is more personal here, but not yet a distinct person.

### New Testament

**Matthew**
- The genealogy runs through Joseph while the virgin birth removes him.
- Sermon on the Mount intensifies the law (thought crime, divorce); NT "relaxation" is Paul, not Jesus.
- "Let the dead bury their dead," "call no one father," the failed "this generation" prophecy (24:34).
- "Son of Man" is a multilayered title; in the parable of the weeds it clearly means Jesus.
- Slavery is accepted; tax collectors were hated as Roman collaborators; baptism has no OT equivalent.

**Mark**
- No birth narrative; named brothers and sisters of Jesus (6:3), which is hard to square with perpetual virginity.
- Jesus's family thought he was out of his mind (3:21).
- "All foods clean" (7:19) is Mark's editorial gloss, which Matthew omits.
- The messianic secret goes wrong comically (can't stay hidden); demon dialogue (Legion) supports a literal reading, which is why only the literalist position is textually consistent.
- "Receive the kingdom as a child" is about manner, not age; marriage ends in the resurrection (12:25).

**Luke and John**
- Passion narratives never mention nails at the crucifixion; only John 20:25 mentions them, afterward.
- Luke 23:34 ("forgive them") is textually uncertain; Stephen's version in Acts 7 may have come first.
- John 17: the prayer of a Son who is "God" to the Father raises the Trinity problem.
- John increasingly blames "the Jews," a trajectory that fed centuries of antisemitism.

**Acts**
- Judas dies by hanging (Matthew) or by rupture (Acts 1); the field-purchase stories conflict.
- Peter's argument for Davidic descent depends on lineage the virgin birth undermines.
- "Hanging on a tree" means the cross (Deut 21:23).
- Acts 10:38 is "low" Christology; the Peter vision reverses dietary law.
- The "we" passages (16:10 onward) are debated: eyewitness, source, or convention.
- Acts 17:29 critiques images, which sits uneasily with church iconography.

**Romans**
- Universal "hearing" (10:18) conflicts with geography; God hardening hearts (11:8) is the recurring setup problem.
- Gentile inclusion is framed as making Israel jealous (11:11-14).
- Romans 12 ("do not repay evil") versus an eye for an eye; Romans 13 ("authorities are God's servants") reverses the Gospel tone.
- Romans 15:9-12 quotes genuine OT passages (Ps 18:49, Deut 32:43, Ps 117:1, Isa 11:10), but Paul builds more from them than they say.
- Tertius (16:22) was the scribe.

**1-2 Corinthians**
- "God's foolishness" (1:25) is deliberate paradox.
- 1 Cor 7: Paul expects the end soon; 8:5-6 states "many gods and many lords"; 14:34-35 (women silent) is probably an interpolation; chapter 13 is about church gifts, not weddings.
- 2 Corinthians is partly a fundraising letter (ch. 8-9) and partly Paul's defense against unnamed "super-apostles."

**Galatians**
- Paul's law-versus-faith contrast fails the logic test you raised: both are claimed revelation, and Christianity's "believe or be lost" is as binary.
- Circumcision is called both fatal and meaningless (5:2-6).
- "Apostle" means "sent one"; Paul claims the title via vision, not acquaintance with Jesus.

**Colossians, Ephesians, 1 Timothy, Titus** (disputed or later)
- Household codes replicate Greco-Roman hierarchy: "equality after death, hierarchy during life."
- 1 Tim 1:10 condemns slave traders, i.e. kidnappers, not slavery. 1 Tim 2:9-15 bars women from teaching.
- The "bishop married once" line (1 Tim 3) and Catholic celibacy; "love of money" is the misquoted "root of evil."
- Titus 1:12 endorses a stereotype of Cretans while chapter 3 says speak evil of no one.

**Hebrews**
- Not Paul's. The "40 years" is the wilderness generation.
- Melchizedek priesthood sets aside descent, but Hebrews itself says Jesus came from Judah, so the factual contradiction is between Matthew and Luke, not Hebrews.
- "If the first covenant had been faultless" (8:7) concedes a flawed first covenant; Hebrews 1 reads comfortably in Arian terms.
- The OT never reveals a Trinity, which believers explain as progressive revelation.

**James, 1-2 Peter, 1 John**
- James: Jesus's brother named matter-of-factly; Catholic reading treats "brother" as kinsman. "Friendship with the world" means the status-driven social order.
- 1 Peter 2:23 "did not threaten" versus Jesus as final judge; the church's ostentation against "God opposes the proud."
- 2 Peter likely dates around 90-150 CE and answers the delay of the Second Coming.
- 1 John: sinlessness (3:6) versus "if we say we have no sin" (1:8); circular test of truth (4:6); a "mortal sin" (5:16-17) that Catholic theology later built on.

**Revelation**
- Author is probably not the apostle; Catholic doctrine doesn't require it.
- "Alpha and Omega" echoes Isaiah's "first and last" and is shared by God and Jesus.
- Jezebel is a nickname for a prophetess in Thyatira; the Laodicea "lukewarm" image reflects the local water supply.
- The four horsemen (third is famine; oil and wine spared as class contrast, not Eucharist).
- Seven means completeness, four means earth, 3.5 means limited evil.
- Two witnesses (11), woman clothed with the sun (12; the church/Israel, not primarily Mary), beast "given" authority (13), the 144,000 (14), the closest NT image of hell (14:9-11).
- Satan is bound and then must be released (20); death ends in the new creation (21:4).

### Other topics

- **Arianism and "Aryan"** are unrelated; the similarity is spelling only.
- **Last Supper**: Leonardo painted Renaissance Italian food, not a Passover seder.
- **"Turn! Turn! Turn!"**, **Tolkien's debt to Maccabees** and **iconoclasm** came up as cultural spin-offs.
- **70 CE** is the destruction of the Second Temple that reshaped both Judaism and Christianity.

## Part 3. Corrections to Earlier Notes

The first version of these notes (written earlier in the conversation) had some errors. Corrected here:

- David's blamelessness hymn is **2 Samuel 22 / Psalm 18**, not Psalm 22.
- The Ecclesiastes items (7:28, "nothing new," "turn turn turn," silver cord, 9:5) are in **Ecclesiastes**, not Proverbs.
- "Prince of princes" (Daniel 8:25) is **God**, not messianic.
- Hosea 6:2 is a **possible** source for the "third day," not a proven one.
- Judges and Joshua, and Samuel's David material, were sometimes mixed under the wrong headings in that version.
